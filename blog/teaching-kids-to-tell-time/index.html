Teaching kids to tell time, step by step

By the FirstFoundationkids team · November 6, 2026

Reading a clock combines numbers, counting by fives and a lot of new words. Breaking it into small steps makes it much easier for children ages 4 to 8.

Step 1: Talk about time in daily life

Before clocks, children learn the order of the day: morning, afternoon, evening and night. Talk about it often: “We eat breakfast in the morning. We go to bed at night.” Words like before, after, first, next and later are the foundation for time.

Step 2: Meet the clock

Show a real analog clock. Point out the numbers 1 to 12 going around, the short hand (the hour hand) and the long hand (the minute hand). A clock with different colored hands helps a lot.

Step 3: O’clock times

Start with the hour hand only. When the long hand points straight up at 12, it is “something o’clock”, and the short hand tells which hour. Practice with times that matter to your child: “At 7 o’clock we have dinner.”

Step 4: Half past

When the long hand points straight down at 6, it is half past the hour. Show how the hour hand has moved halfway to the next number. Cutting a paper circle in half helps children see why it is called “half” past.

Step 5: Quarter past and quarter to

Fold the paper circle into quarters. Quarter past is when the long hand is at 3, and quarter to is when it is at 9. In some countries, children learn to say “7:15” and “7:45” instead, which is fine too.

Step 6: Counting by fives

Each number on the clock stands for 5 minutes when you read the long hand. Practice counting by fives around the clock: 5, 10, 15, 20… Once children can count by fives, they can read times like 3:25.

Step 7: Digital clocks

Show how the same time looks on a digital clock. Children often find digital easier to read, but analog clocks help them understand how time passes.

What age do children learn this?

It varies by country. In the United States, for example, the Common Core standards introduce telling time to the hour and half hour in first grade, to the nearest five minutes in second grade, and to the nearest minute in third grade. Many children enjoy starting with o’clock times earlier, especially with a clock at home to look at.

Everyday ideas

  • Put an analog clock where your child can see it every day.
  • Ask “What time is it?” at big moments like lunch and bath time.
  • Use a visual timer for activities, so children feel how long 10 minutes is.
  • Make a paper clock with movable hands and set times for each other.
