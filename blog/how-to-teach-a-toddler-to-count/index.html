How to teach a toddler to count

By the FirstFoundationkids team · November 6, 2026

Counting is much more than saying “one, two, three”. Here is how the skill really grows between ages 2 and 4, and ten everyday games that help it along.

Counting grows in small steps

When a two-year-old proudly says “one, two, three, five, ten!”, they are not getting it wrong. They are at the first step. Counting is really several skills that come together over a few years:

  1. Saying the number words in order. At first this is like a song. Children learn the sound of “one, two, three” before they know what it means.
  2. One number for each thing. Touching each object once and saying one number for it. This is called one-to-one correspondence, and it takes lots of practice.
  3. The last number tells how many. Understanding that when you count “one, two, three” cookies, there are three cookies. This idea, called cardinality, is a big leap.
  4. Seeing small amounts at a glance. Knowing there are three dots without counting them one by one. This is called subitizing.
  5. Matching amounts to written numbers. Knowing that the shape “3” means three things.

Most children work on steps 1 and 2 around age 2 to 3, and steps 3 to 5 between 3 and 5. Every child moves at their own pace, so use these as a rough guide, not a test.

10 playful ways to practice counting

  1. Count the stairs. Say a number for every step you climb together. Moving the body while counting makes one-to-one counting easier.
  2. Snack counting. Put a few crackers or berries on a plate. Count them together, then ask “How many?” Eat one and count again.
  3. Sing number songs. Rhymes like “Five little ducks” or “One, two, buckle my shoe” teach the order of number words.
  4. Touch and count. Line up toy cars or blocks. Help your child touch each one as you count. Pushing each toy to one side after counting it stops double counting.
  5. Give me three. Ask for a small number of things: “Can you give me three spoons?” This checks whether your child knows that “three” means an amount.
  6. Dot cards and dice. Roll a die and ask how many dots. Over time children start to “just see” 1, 2 and 3 without counting.
  7. Count out loud during routines. Count buttons on a coat, socks in the laundry, or how many people are at the table.
  8. More or fewer? Make two small piles and ask which has more. Comparing amounts builds number sense.
  9. Point out written numbers. House numbers, buses, clocks and lifts are full of numbers to spot and say.
  10. Short game sessions. A few minutes of a well-designed counting game can add variety, especially if it says the numbers out loud and gives gentle help when a child is unsure.

Tips that make counting stick

  • Keep numbers small at first. Counting to 5 confidently is worth more than reciting to 20.
  • Count real things. Children understand amounts best when they can touch and move them.
  • Make mistakes okay. If your child skips a number, count again together instead of saying “wrong”.
  • Ask “How many?” after counting. It helps children connect the last number with the total.
  • Stop while it is still fun. Little and often works better than long sessions.

When to talk to someone

Children learn counting at very different speeds. If you have any worries about your child’s development, your pediatrician, family doctor, health visitor or preschool teacher is the best person to ask.
