How to teach the alphabet and letter sounds

By the FirstFoundationkids team · November 6, 2026

Knowing letters is one of the strongest building blocks for learning to read. The good news: it can be taught through play, a few minutes at a time.

Letter names and letter sounds are different

Every letter has a name (“bee”) and a sound (“b” as in ball). Children need both. The letter name helps them talk about letters, and the letter sound is what they use to read and spell words. Many reading programs around the world, including phonics programs used in schools, teach letter sounds early because they unlock reading.

A simple order that works for many children

  1. Start with their name. The letters in a child’s own name are the most exciting letters in the world. Start there.
  2. Capitals first, then lowercase. Capital letters are easier to tell apart. Lowercase letters like b, d, p and q are easy to mix up, so give them extra time.
  3. Add sounds as you go. When you show the letter M, say both “This is M” and “It says mmm, like moon.”
  4. Listen for first sounds. Play “I spy something that starts with sss.” Hearing sounds in words, called phonological awareness, is just as important as seeing letters.

Games for the alphabet

  • Letter hunt. Pick a letter of the day and find it on cereal boxes, signs and books.
  • Sound sorting. Put a few toys on the floor and sort them by first sound: ball and bear in one pile, car and cat in another.
  • Write in sand or shaving foam. Big finger movements help children remember letter shapes before they hold a pencil well.
  • Alphabet songs, slowly. The alphabet song is fun, but sing it slowly while pointing at letters, so “L-M-N-O-P” does not sound like one long word.
  • Read alphabet books together. Stop and ask “What sound does this start with?”
  • Match capital to lowercase. Write capitals on one set of cards and lowercase on another, then play a matching game.

How fast should it go?

There is no single right age. Some three-year-olds know many letters, and some five-year-olds are just getting started. What matters most is regular, happy practice. A few minutes a day of looking at letters, saying sounds and reading together adds up quickly.

Signs your child is ready for the next step

  • They can name most capital letters and many lowercase letters.
  • They can tell you the first sound of simple words like “sun” or “map”.
  • They start trying to “sound out” short words or spell their name.

When you see these, it may be a good time to start sight words and simple reading together.
